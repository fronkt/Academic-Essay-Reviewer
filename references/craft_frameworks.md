## Part 1 — College Essay Guy (Ethan Sawyer), collegeessayguy.com

Source note: the free blog posts and the free Personal Statement guide contain the **full**
frameworks, not teasers — every step, chart, and example cited below was read on a public page.
Paid courses and 1:1 coaching exist but are not required to use any method here.

### 1.1 Structure — the two shapes, and how to choose · VERIFIED

Source: https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/how-to-write-a-college-essay

The core distinction, verbatim: **"In Narrative Structure, story events connect *chronologically*.
In Montage Structure, story events connect *thematically*."**

**The choosing rule** is two questions, not one:
1. Have you faced significant challenges in your life?
2. Do you *want* to write about them?

Both yes → narrative is available. Otherwise → montage. His stated default when unsure:
*"I generally recommend experimenting with montage first; you can always go back and play with narrative."*

> **Reader use.** Do not grade an essay against the wrong shape. A montage essay is not "unfocused"
> for lacking a chronology, and a narrative essay is not "linear" as a criticism. Name which shape
> the draft is attempting, then grade whether it executes *that* shape. An essay attempting neither
> — a chronology with no turn and no thread — is the actual finding.

**Narrative beats** (5-beat form): Status Quo → Inciting Incident → Raising the Stakes →
Moment of Truth (a choice is made) → New Status Quo. Coarser thirds form:
Challenges + Effects | What I Did About It | What I Learned.

**Montage structure** · VERIFIED · https://www.collegeessayguy.com/guides/the-free-guide/narrative-essay
(note: the URL slug says "narrative-essay" but the page is about montage)

*"A montage is, simply put, a series of moments or story events connected by a common thematic thread."*
Build: **beads** (moments/experiences, usually one per paragraph) strung on a **thread** (the unifying theme).
Process: pick a topic you know thoroughly → brainstorm the values it lets you show → find those same
values manifesting elsewhere in your life.

The governing principle, verbatim: *"Colleges care less about your topic… and more about **you**…
Your topic is basically an excuse to talk about all the qualities you will bring to the campus community."*

> **Beyond the two shapes.** Segmented, braided, refrain, insert, and borrowed-document forms, with
> the evidence on when they work, are in `creative_forms.md`. Narrative and montage remain the default:
> 105 of the 118 showcase essays the skill counted are plain prose (`creative_forms.md` §1).

### 1.2 Brainstorming exercises · all VERIFIED

Run these in `brainstorm` mode. They are ordered from lowest to highest emotional demand — start
where the writer is comfortable, never open with Feelings & Needs.

| Exercise | What it asks | What it surfaces | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Everything I Want Colleges to Know About Me** | ~20 min. Bullet-list everything you want colleges to know; back general claims with specific examples. Then route each item to a destination: personal statement / activities list / additional info / supplement | A raw inventory routed across the *whole application*, not just one essay. Explicitly NOT a stream-of-consciousness freewrite, which "brings forth a lot of words but not a lot of specific, bullet-pointable qualities" | [blog/2014/12/2/…](https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/2014/12/2/the-everything-i-want-colleges-to-know-about-me-list-a-brainstorm-exercise) |
| **21 Details** | *"Make a list of 21 details from your life, interesting facts that describe some small, random part of who you are."* Explicitly not achievements or awards. Then look for recurring themes | Quiet, idiosyncratic, unrepeatable material — the opposite of résumé content | [blog/brainstorm-21-details](https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/brainstorm-21-details) |
| **Essence Objects** | Choose physical objects of personal significance — *"not a trophy"* — guided by ~18 prompts ("What is an object that reminds you of home?", "What is in your bag right now?") | Moves the writer "from vague feelings ('I love music') to concrete, specific experiences." Direct antidote to the sensory-detail absence in `ai_tells.md` §C | [blog/the-objects-exercise-video-walkthrough](https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/the-objects-exercise-video-walkthrough) |
| **Values Exercise** | Funnel a 100+ value list: pick 10 → narrow to 5 → choose top 3. Then find the moments that demonstrate each | Framed as the antidote to "the common trap of writing a topic-focused essay — one that describes an activity without revealing the writer's inner life" | [blog/the-values-exercise](https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/the-values-exercise) |
| **Feelings & Needs** | An 8-column chart built on Nonviolent Communication: **Challenges → Effects → Feelings → Needs → What I Did About It → Lessons Learned → Skills & Values → (optional) Future**. "Effects" is explicitly *not* emotions yet; "Needs" is the longest step and is called "the heart of this exercise" | The material for a narrative-structure essay, arrived at honestly rather than by reverse-engineering a lesson. Note: *"Mixed emotions are normal and noting them can actually make for a more interesting, nuanced personal statement"* | [blog/feelings-needs-exercise](https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/feelings-needs-exercise) |
| **Roles and Identities** | Pick 10 identities → narrow to 5 → for each, list 2–5 concrete manifestations (moving from "I am a maker" to "using an Arduino to create a color-changing lamp") → research how each could thrive on the campus → connect 1–3 to specific opportunities | Built for identity/community/contribution supplements | [blog/identity-diversity-contribute-essays-roles-and-identities](https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/identity-diversity-contribute-essays-roles-and-identities) |

### 1.3 Supplement frameworks · VERIFIED

**"Why us?"** — https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/why-this-college-essay and
https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/dos-and-donts-why-us-statement

Research method: the school's own site and **course catalog**; expert guides (Fiske, *Colleges That
Change Lives*, Princeton Review); student reviews; virtual tours; talking to reps and current students;
and one specific named move — **find and read an actual class syllabus** for a course you might take,
to see what a professor really emphasizes. (This is the highest-leverage item on the list and the one
almost no applicant does.)

His named traps, from the Dos/Don'ts page:
- Generic praise that fits any school ("Being around sophisticated and intellectual students…would inspire me")
- Naming programs the school obviously knows it has — verbatim: *"SMU KNOWS WHAT PROGRAMS IT HAS; WHICH PROGRAMS ARE YOU INTERESTED IN AND WHY?"*
- Universal claims ("professors are dedicated to helping students succeed")
- Insufficient research
- Fix: name exact courses and professors (*"Biblical Hebrew"* not just *"Hebrew"*), and tie each to something you have actually done

His formula: **A (school-specific detail) + B (how it connects back to you) = a usable "Why us?" sentence.**

> **Terminology caution.** The "would this sentence survive find-and-replace with another school's
> name?" test is a real and useful operationalization of what he teaches, but **"find-and-replace test"
> and "any-school test" are OUR labels, not his branded terms.** The `institutional_reader_agent`
> already runs this count — keep running it, attribute the *concept* to him, not the *name*.

**BEABIES** (activity / extracurricular essays) · CORROBORATED
Expansion: *"the Best Extracurricular Activity Brainstorm I've Ever Seen."* One chart per candidate
activity: **What I Did | Problems I Solved | Lessons Learned / Skills & Values | Impact (on self, school,
community, society) | How I Applied It Elsewhere.** Each column can become a sentence or two of a first draft.
Status note: the acronym and column set were consistently corroborated across sources including CEG's own
site language, but the full chart lives in a linked Google Doc that would not render, so this entry is
CORROBORATED rather than directly quoted. Referenced from
https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/college-essay-prompts#E

**"Why major?"** · VERIFIED · https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/why-major-college-essay
Named technique: the **"mini-movie" outline** — bullet the moments that led to the interest, arrange
them chronologically, then decide where the thesis sits (beginning, middle, or end). Length rules:
at 100–150 words, one scene per *sentence*; at 250–300, one scene per *short paragraph*. Also:
the major essay must **complement, not contradict**, the personal statement; and in short essays
*"prioritize content over poetry."* Undecided students pick 1–3 interests and trace curiosity in each.

**Community / identity** · VERIFIED · https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/community-essay
Four steps: (1) choose the community using a **Communities Chart** organized by
**Place · Action · Interest · Circumstance**; (2) run BEABIES on it; (3) pick narrative or montage —
montage paired with the **Uncommon Connections Exercise**: list the values other applicants would
predictably claim for that community, then brainstorm 3–4 values they would *not* think of, each with
a specific example; (4) draft.

### 1.4 Craft heuristics · VERIFIED

- **The specificity principle** (the single most quotable line in the corpus, and the best one-sentence
  statement of what separates a 5 from a 3): write about *"ordinary things with extraordinary specificity,
  rather than extraordinary events with ordinary reflection."*
- **"So what?" test** — ask it of each example; if no specific insight emerges, the example is decoration.
- **Uncommon Connections** — *"The more common your topic is … the more uncommon your connections need
  to be if you want to stand out."* His example: a boxing essay that demonstrated humor, experimentation,
  intellectual vitality, quick thinking, patience and critical thinking instead of the predictable
  grit/hard-work/perseverance triad. **This is the operational salvage mechanism for `cliche_taxonomy.md`** —
  when the DA finds a cliché arc, "uncommon connections" is the concrete direction that rescues it.
- **Show, don't tell** · https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/show-dont-tell — *"if you are going to err on
  one side or the other, err on the side of over-showing."* Techniques: sensory detail, specific proper nouns,
  active verbs, data, feelings language. His before/after: telling — *"I harness salient people skills to
  connect deeply with others"*; showing — *"Because I am quite curious, I often engage with customers at my
  Target register who chat with me about their beloved, rambunctious grandkids."*
  ⚠️ There is **no 70/30 show-tell ratio** in his material — that number circulates on other sites. Do not cite it.
- **Hooks** · https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/write-good-hook-for-essay — strong hooks *"create questions
  in the reader's mind rather than answering them"* and must be *"specific to the writer — a detail or moment
  so particular that it could not belong to anyone else."* Named worst hooks: famous quotations, grand
  statements about humanity, "as long as I can remember" openings. (Cross-ref `cliche_taxonomy.md` #16, #17.)
- **The Great College Essay Test 2.0** · https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/tgcet — a self-scoring pass.
  Four qualities of a strong personal essay: **Core Values · Vulnerability · "So what" moments · Craft.**
  Rough bar: a reader should be able to name **4–5 of the writer's core values** and find **3–5 "so what"
  moments**; 3 of 4 qualities is a pass. Five "admission nutrients": intellectual curiosity, service/community
  impact, leadership or initiative, collaboration, consistent engagement.

### 1.5 His named anti-patterns · VERIFIED
https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/what-not-to-write-college-essay

Résumé rehash · mission trip · sports challenge · the big performance · cliché immigration story ·
"why X is my hero" (*"the essay ends up being more about the other person than yourself"*) · the tough
grade you got · first heartbreak · illegal or unethical activity (*"the only outcome of such an essay is
making the admissions committee question how long it will take you to start cheating"*).

These **corroborate `cliche_taxonomy.md` from an independent source** — where an arc appears in both,
the DA may say so, which strengthens the finding beyond one reader's impression.

**Trauma** · https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/trauma-in-college-essays — stated in caps on his page:
*"YOU DO NOT HAVE TO WRITE ABOUT TRAUMATIC EXPERIENCES TO WRITE A GREAT PERSONAL STATEMENT."*
If a challenge is included, make it *"a chapter"* of the essay, not *"the whole book."*
(Cross-ref `cliche_taxonomy.md` #8 and `essay_type_rubrics.md` #7.)

## Part 2 — Cross-source corroboration

The strongest craft claims are the ones an independent admissions office states *and* an independent
practitioner states. When a reader can cite both, the finding stops being taste and starts being consensus.

| Claim | Practitioner source | Institutional corroboration (`university_guidance.md`) |
|---|---|---|
| The topic does not matter; the handling does | CEG: "ordinary things with extraordinary specificity" | Yale, Harvard, Vanderbilt, JHU, Hamilton — all state it independently |
| Depth over breadth; one moment, not a survey | CEG: montage beads are specific; "so what" per example | Tufts, Carleton (*"focus on one event"*) |
| Do not restate the résumé | CEG anti-pattern #1 | Penn, UC (*"don't make it a list of accomplishments"*), Dartmouth |
| Voice must be recognizably yours | CEG: specificity "could not belong to anyone else" | Penn's recognition test, Vanderbilt's, Tufts, Georgia Tech |
| Over-editing destroys voice | CEG warns against consultant-shaped prose | Tufts, Carleton, Hamilton, Georgia Tech, UVA — five independent offices |

Where practitioner advice and an institution's stated preference **conflict**, the institution governs
for that school, and the report says which source it is following and why.
